### Step 1: Identify Business Activities and Competitive Overlaps
Not all mergers equal. Important how they impact market.
1. Horizontal (between competitors) - high risk
2. Vertical (between supplier-customer) - mid risk but can raise concerns about #foreclosure (denying rivals access to inputs or customers)
3. Conglomerate (separate but related markets) - low risk;

Work at this step - Map out all products and services by each company and pinpoint any horizontal overlaps (where products/services compete) or vertical links (where one product's/service's output is another's input).

### Step 2: Define Relevant Product and Geographic Markets (Using Precedents)

2 key things:
1. Identifying markets (geo and product)
2. Looking at precedent decisions

#####  Looking at precedent decisions
- Research previous cases in the same industry or with similar issues and see regulator decisions
- Competition agencies often follow established definitions from earlier decisions, so these can be compiled as benchmarks;
- Ultimately, relevant market is based on substitutability (precedents are not absolute!);

### Step 3: Calculate Market Shares Under Different Market Definitions

- Calculate market share of Firm A, Firm B and Firm A+B, typically using sales or volume data;
- Do this for each plausible market definition, both narrow (e.g single product) and broadly.
- Evaluate best-case and worst-case outcomes as regulator don't settle on one definition early on.

### Step 4: Evaluate Horizontal and Vertical Competitive Effects
##### Horizontal effects
- Primary concern - loss of competition
- Asses if merger allows **unilaterally raise prices or reduce quality/innovation**
- Check how competitive the firms were and what happens after merger;
- #Coordinated-effects - asses if after the merger it is easier for firms to coordinate behavior rather than compete.
- #Maverick: if one of the firms was aggressively competing and undercut prices, removing such makes market much more prone to quiet coordination.

##### Vertical effects
- #Foreclosure: can merged firm disadvantage its rivals by leveraging presence in one market to strengthen its position in another
	- Input foreclosure: ability and incentive to block input reaching competitors;
	- Customer foreclosure: limit customers reaching competitors;
- Problem when one company has **significant market power in one of both of the vertically related markets**.
